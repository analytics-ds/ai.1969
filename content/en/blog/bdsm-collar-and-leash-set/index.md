## 1. The day collar, the one worn outside {#day-collar}

The day collar is a thin choker or a steel ring that no outside observer reads as BDSM gear. Its function is symbolic: it marks a bond that continues outside sessions.

Check the clasp first, because a collar worn all day catches on collars, scarves and seat belts. A clasp that releases under a firm pull is a feature here, not a flaw. Then the material, stainless steel or thin leather, both of which tolerate prolonged skin contact. This type of collar rarely takes a heavy leash. The ring is often decorative and the assembly is not built to absorb traction. For a complete set, the next family is the right starting point.

## 2. The leather session collar, the default choice {#session-collar}

This is the model that covers the widest range of situations, and the one to pick for a first **collar and leash set**. Wide, adjustable, lined or padded, it carries one or more D-rings that take a leash as easily as wrist cuffs.

Three criteria matter. Width, between three and five centimetres, because a narrow band concentrates pressure on a line instead of spreading it. Lining, meaning a genuine stitched lining rather than a folded flap of leather. And ring quality, welded rather than simply bent closed, since the ring takes the entire pull.

## 3. The posture collar, for advanced practice only {#posture-collar}

Tall and rigid, it runs from the top of the sternum to the jaw and restricts head movement. It does not squeeze the throat, it prevents lowering the chin. That is a postural restraint, not a respiratory one, and the distinction is essential.

A posture collar is not worn while walking down stairs, not in public, and never without direct supervision.

## 4. Choosing the leash that goes with it {#leash}

A **BDSM leash** is chosen on three points, and the strap is not one of them.

The snap hook first. That is the part that breaks. A cast zamak hook fails, a solid steel or brass hook holds. It must open one-handed, including by the person wearing the collar.

Length next. A short leash, around fifty centimetres, guides at close range, almost in contact. A metre-long leash allows walking. Those are different uses, and an over-long leash drags, snags and becomes a fall risk.

Material last. Leather is comfortable in the hand and silent. Chain has weight, sound, and transmits every movement. That is an atmosphere choice as much as a comfort one.

## 5. Leather, steel or rope: what the material changes {#material}

Real leather develops a patina, relaxes slightly with use and moulds to the neck. It is the most comfortable material over a long session, and the only one that needs maintenance.

Steel does not relax. It suits the day collar and the ownership collar, less so a multi-hour session where metal eventually marks the skin. It fears neither water nor cleaning.

Rope is soft, light and consistent with shibari practice. It does tighten under traction, which rules out clipping a leash pulled with any force.

On sensitive or reactive skin, go for a lined model and avoid direct metal-on-skin contact beyond a few minutes.

## 6. Safety: what is not negotiable {#safety}

The neck is the most fragile area on which BDSM places equipment. The rules below are not comfort recommendations.

Two fingers must slide between the collar and the neck without force, with the collar closed. That is the only sizing check that counts, before every session and not just on the first wear.

No pull on the leash should tighten the throat. A leash guides, slows and steers. It never pulls upward or backward. A collar and leash make no breath-play practice safe, and no equipment ever will.

A collar is never left on a sleeping person, nor on a person left alone, even loosened. The thin steel day collar is the single exception some practitioners allow themselves, and it stays a decision to discuss beforehand, not a norm.

A safe word is agreed before starting, with a non-verbal backup signal for when a gag or a posture collar makes speech difficult. ## 7. Caring for leather and metal {#care}

A dry cloth after each use is enough in most cases. Leather needs a conditioning balm two or three times a year, never alcohol or solvent-based cleaner, which dries it out and cracks it. Dry it flat, away from a radiator.

Steel is cleaned with soapy water and dried immediately to avoid marks. Ropes are hand-washed and dried under tension, never coiled while damp.

Storage matters as much as cleaning: away from light, flat or hanging, not folded in a bag where leather takes a permanent crease.

<summary>What is the difference between a day collar and a session collar?</summary>

The day collar is discreet and worn in public, often a thin choker or a steel ring with symbolic value. The session collar is wider, sturdier, lined, and built to take a leash or restraints during play. The first is worn for hours, the second comes off at the end of the session.

<summary>How do you know if a BDSM collar fits correctly?</summary>

Two fingers must slide between the collar and the neck without force, once the collar is closed. Tighter and it compresses the windpipe and blood vessels. Looser and it rotates, chafes and eventually marks. Multi-buckle models adjust, which remains the safest choice for a first purchase.

<summary>Which leash goes with which collar?</summary>

Any snap-hook leash clips onto a D-ring. The only two things to check are the ring diameter, which must accept the hook opening, and the strength of that hook. A leash never attaches to an adjustment buckle or a decorative ring. Sets sold as matching pairs remove the question entirely.

<details>
<summary>Can you sleep wearing a BDSM collar?</summary>

No. A collar is never left on a sleeping person or someone left alone, even loosened, because movement during sleep can rotate it and compress the neck. Thin steel ownership collars are the only exception some couples allow, and only after a tested period of waking wear.
